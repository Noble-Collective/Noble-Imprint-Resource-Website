## **Introduction**

The Christian life is based upon correct beliefs about what is true, good, and beautiful in the world. In fact, confession of truth determines one’s ability to enjoy life as God has designed it. Believing the *truth* enables us to know what is *good*; knowing the *good* unlocks our enjoyment of the *beautiful*. This is especially important as the world in which we live is frightfully cursed: the truth is combatted with error, the good is ruined by sin, and the beautiful is vandalized by evil. Outside of Christ, there are only lies, brokenness, and despair. However, in Christ we are saved from falsehood into the light of truth. The Christian life is a redeemed life, a life rescued from sin, condemnation, evil, and eternal punishment. Saved to new life in Christ, believers are delivered from the disastrous consequences of living based upon the false pretenses of an untrue narrative of the world. Rooted in the teachings of inspired Scripture, Christian disciples know and embrace *the true story of the world*.

The selected Bible passages in this study offer a brief sketch of the true story of the world. In Job 1:1–2:13, the ancient testimony of a God-fearer named Job exemplifies genuine faith in a broken world. Returning to the origins of the world, the creation account in Genesis 1:1–2:3 reveals the nature of God as sovereign and supreme Creator of all things. The biblical record in Genesis 2:4–25 attests to the nature, purpose, and character of humanity, the crown of God’s creative work. The tragic account in Genesis 3:1–24 narrates the temptation and rebellion of the first humans which led to the ruin of God’s perfect world. A historical narration of a devastating flood, Genesis 5:28–9:17 showcases God’s promise of judgment and salvation. The sum of all divine promises was fulfilled in the coming of Christ. The miraculous birth of the eternal Son of God in the form of human flesh—the incarnation—is told in Luke 1:1–2:21 and it teaches us about the person and work of Christ. Christ’s earthly ministry culminated in betrayal, suffering, death, burial, and resurrection. This is chronicled in Mark 15:1–16:8.

In Acts 1:1–2:47, we read about Jesus’ heavenly ascension and the coming of the Holy Spirit. The biblical story in Acts 10:1–11:18 teaches us about conversion—the transformation of those who place their faith in Christ—by describing the salvation of Cornelius and his household. On the island of Patmos, John recorded a vision of Jesus and his seven letters to seven churches. This vision is faithfully detailed in Revelation 1:9–3:22 and it teaches us about the nature of the church: the community of Christ-followers. The vivid and graphic vision in Revelation 12:1–14:20 instructs us about the nature of God’s kingdom: God’s victorious and triumphant rule over all things, especially the forces of darkness. The last chapters of the Bible, Revelation 19:11–22:21, are a faithful foretelling of Christian hope: the return of Christ, the defeat of Satan, the judgment of evil, the resurrection of the body, and the new creation of the world.

As divine revelation to humanity, the biblical story is a faithful narrative of God’s dealings with his creation. In essence, the Bible tells us *the story behind it all*. It teaches us about the beginnings of the world, the tragic ruin of original creation, and God’s plan to rescue his people from eternal condemnation. It accurately records the past, powerfully speaks to the present, and precisely predicts the future. In many ways, the Bible is a book about the one and only true God—God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Spirit. But, because the Bible is a book about the true God, it is also a book about everything else that is true, meaningful, and important in life. Inasmuch as the biblical story records God’s thoughts to mankind—truth about the past, present, and future—the biblical story teaches *fundamental doctrines that shape life*. Only when we understand the true story behind it all can we embrace the substance of life as God intended it to be.
